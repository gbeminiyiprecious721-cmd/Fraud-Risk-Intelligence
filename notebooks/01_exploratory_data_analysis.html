Stage 3 — Exploratory Data Analysis¶

Fraud Risk Intelligence & Decision Support System¶

Business context (full detail in docs/PROBLEM_STATEMENT.md): this project simulates a fraud-risk function at a hypothetical payment processor. The primary decision supported is prioritizing transactions for human investigation under a capacity-constrained queue — not automated decline-at-checkout (explicitly out of scope). Evaluation will use Average Precision (AP), precision/recall at a fixed capacity, and cost-sensitive thresholding (Stage 10).

Dataset context (full detail in docs/DATA_VALIDATION_REPORT.md): IEEE-CIS Fraud Detection (Kaggle). Stage 2 confirmed: 590,540 train transactions (394 cols, incl. isFraud), a chronological train/test holdout (test starts ~30 days after train ends), 24.42% train / 28.01% test identity-join coverage with zero orphan rows, and a train_identity/test_identity naming mismatch (id_XX vs. id-XX) carried forward as a Stage 5 prerequisite.

Research questions for this stage:

  1. How imbalanced is the target, and what does that imply for metric/model choice?
  2. How do transaction amounts differ between fraud and legitimate transactions?
  3. Does fraud rate vary across the observed time window (in relative terms only — no calendar dates)?
  4. Is missingness structural or a quality problem, and does it differ by fraud status?
  5. Which categorical/numerical features show a measured association with the target — and which anonymized features remain an open leakage question?
  6. Does identity-data availability relate to fraud prevalence (without assuming it causes it)?

Scope boundary: structural/distributional EDA only. No fitted imputers/scalers/encoders, no target-based feature selection, no train/test split, no modeling. That's Stage 4+.

Memory strategy: every analysis below reads only the 1-8 columns it needs — at ~590K rows, a column-selective read is a few MB regardless of row count, which already bounds memory well below what row-sampling would save, while staying exact. The one place that genuinely needs chunking is the full-width (394-column) missingness scan in Section D. Row-sampling was considered and not used — see the writeup in docs/EDA_REPORT.md for why.

Verifying the sign flip (added on review — why does d change sign?)¶

Cohen's d is a location (central-tendency) statistic. If the raw-vs-log sign flip were just "skew distorting the mean," that's a shape argument, not yet a verified one — check it directly instead of asserting it.

Verification result: the geometric-mean-like typical amount is higher for legit, not fraud (computed above) — consistent with the negative log-scale d. The Q3/Q1 ratio is substantially wider for fraud than for legit (computed above) — fraud transactions are more dispersed in both directions (a lower Q1 and a higher Q3), not shifted to one side. Fraud also shows a higher share of very small transactions (computed above), consistent with a card-testing-style pattern (small verification charges before a larger attempt) coexisting with occasional larger fraud amounts.

This changes the interpretation, not just the number: the real difference between the groups isn't a location shift that Cohen's d (built for exactly that) is designed to summarize — it's a dispersion/shape difference. Reporting either d value alone, raw or log, answers "is the center different" (not much) to a question that was actually "is the spread different" (yes, considerably). The right tool for that question is a spread ratio (Q3/Q1, or a variance ratio), not an effect size built for location shifts — a case where picking the statistic that matches the shape of the actual difference matters more than picking the "more correct" scale for a statistic that was the wrong choice either way.

Figures B1/B2 — interpretation

  • Question: do fraudulent and legitimate transactions differ in amount, and by how much?
  • Finding: both groups' descriptive stats are printed above. The raw-scale effect size and the log1p-scale effect size don't just differ in magnitude — they point in different directions (one positive, one slightly negative), and both are well below even a "small" effect by Cohen's benchmark (|d| ≈ 0.2). Read that as: transaction amount alone is not a strong fraud signal in this data, on either scale — the raw-scale mean gap is driven more by a few large fraud-side outliers (visible in the wider fraud IQR printed above) than by a systematic size difference.
  • Why it matters: if amount separates the classes at all, it's a candidate feature; if the effect is small, amount alone won't carry much signal and interaction/engineered features will matter more.
  • Limitations (stated explicitly, as required): Cohen's d assumes roughly symmetric distributions with comparable variances — TransactionAmt is right-skewed (visible in B1), so the raw-scale d can be distorted by a few extreme values; the log-scale d is more trustworthy here but changes the units of the comparison (log-dollars, not dollars) — a real amount difference at the low end and the high end of the distribution are NOT weighted equally once log-transformed. Neither d tells you which transactions are driving the difference, and this is an association, not a causal claim about why fraud correlates with amount.
  • Modeling implication: a log or power transform of TransactionAmt is worth testing in Stage 5/6; outlier counts (IQR method, printed above) inform whether capping/winsorizing is worth considering — a Stage 5 decision, not applied here.

G. Leakage Investigation — Open Questions Preserved for Stage 4¶

This section is deliberately narrative, building on findings above rather than introducing new charts. Two concrete, specific findings from this stage are carried forward as open questions — named directly, not left as a generic placeholder:

  1. R_emaildomain and a block of ~14 V-columns (V180, V184, V185, V188, V189, V194, V195, V197, V198, V200, V201, V208, V209, V210) are all ~30-31 percentage points less missing for fraud transactions than legitimate ones (Section D). These columns moving together, by nearly identical gap sizes, suggests they're populated by a shared upstream process (plausibly tied to whether a receiver/shipping email domain was captured) — not evidence of leakage by itself, but a strong, specific, named pattern worth Stage 4 checking empirically (e.g., is R_emaildomain's presence itself determined by something only knowable after the transaction was flagged?), rather than a vague "some anonymized columns might leak."
  2. Identity-data presence correlates with a ~3.7x higher fraud rate (7.85% vs. 2.09%, Section F) — large enough that whether identity collection itself is triggered by a prior risk signal (which would make has_identity leakage-adjacent) is now a specific, answerable question for Stage 4, not a hypothetical one.
  3. The remaining anonymized V/C/D columns (368 total, per Stage 2) are still uncleared — nothing in this stage's analysis resolves whether any were computed non-causally. The open question from Stage 2 remains open.
  4. train_identity/test_identity naming mismatch (id_XX vs. id-XX, Stage 2): not a leakage issue, but still unresolved — repeated here so it isn't lost before Stage 5.

Nothing found this stage overturns Stage 2's findings — this stage's numbers extend Stage 2's (e.g. the fraud-prevalence figure in Section A upgrades the externally-known ~3.5% rate from [EXTERNAL, UNCONFIRMED] to [VERIFIED]), it's additional detail, not a contradiction.

Key Conclusions and Implications for Subsequent Stages¶

  1. Class imbalance confirmed and quantified: 3.499% fraud prevalence, a 27.6:1 imbalance ratio (Section A, now [VERIFIED] rather than externally-known-but-unconfirmed) — validates Stage 1's metric choices (AP, precision/recall@K, cost-sensitive thresholding over accuracy).
  2. Transaction amount is NOT a strong standalone fraud signal — both raw- and log-scale effect sizes are near zero and disagree in sign (Section B). Don't let this project's narrative overstate amount's importance just because it's the most intuitive feature.
  3. Temporal fraud rate is noisy but stable around the overall 3.5% rate across the observed window (Section C, std ≈1.0 percentage point across 182 relative days) — informs Stage 4's split and Stage 18's monitoring design.
  4. Missingness is informative, not just a nuisance, and one pattern stands out concretely: R_emaildomain and ~14 V-columns are ~30pp less missing for fraud transactions (Section D) — a named, specific lead for Stage 4/6, not a vague one.
  5. A ranked, reliability-flagged set of categorical and numeric candidates exists for Stage 7 baseline features (Section E): ProductCD, R_emaildomain, and M4 lead on Cramér's V; D1 leads the low-missingness numeric set on Cohen's d — with explicit caveats about Cramér's V bias and the missingness-first numeric selection's own blind spot.
  6. Identity-data availability shows a large, counter-intuitive fraud-rate gap (7.85% vs. 2.09%, ~3.7x, Section F) — strong enough that it's now a leakage question, not a clean feature, pending Stage 4.
  7. No leakage question was resolved or dismissed (Section G) — two specific, named findings (the V-column/email-domain missingness pattern and the identity-coverage gap) plus the two carried-over items from Stage 2, all explicitly open going into Stage 4.

Limitations of This Notebook¶

  • All numeric effect sizes (Cohen's d, Cramér's V) are descriptive summaries, not significance tests — deliberate, given the large-sample p-value problem noted when dependencies were requested for this stage, but it means "no effect size reported" categories/columns were excluded for low n, not evaluated and found null.
  • The numeric-feature selection in Section E used a missingness-first criterion decided before checking fraud association, which avoids cherry-picking but necessarily excludes several high-missingness columns that Stage 2 already flagged as potentially informative.
  • TransactionDT's absolute real-world meaning remains unconfirmed (Stage 2) — all temporal findings here are in relative terms only, by design.
  • Nothing here establishes feature availability at inference time for the anonymized columns — that is explicitly Stage 4's job.
